What is Quantum Physics?

Quantum physics is the branch of science that studies the behavior of matter and energy at the atomic and subatomic levels. While classical Newtonian physics can explain the motion of large objects, it fails to interpret the laws governing the microscopic world, leading to the birth of quantum physics. In this realm, energy is not a continuous flow, but is exchanged in small, discrete packets or units called 'quanta'.

Key Characteristics and Concepts

  • Wave-Particle Duality: The unusual ability of subatomic particles like electrons and photons to exhibit properties of both waves and particles simultaneously.
  • Heisenberg's Uncertainty Principle: Proposed by Werner Heisenberg, this principle states that it is impossible to simultaneously measure both the exact position and momentum of a particle with absolute precision.
  • Quantum Entanglement: A mysterious connection between two or more particles where the state of one particle instantly influences the other, regardless of the distance separating them.
  • Superposition: The ability of a quantum system to exist in a combination of multiple states or probabilities simultaneously until a measurement is made.

History and Development

  • Basic Origins (1900): Max Planck's proposal that energy is exchanged in discrete packets to explain blackbody radiation, marking the birth of quantum theory.
  • Photoelectric Effect (1905): Albert Einstein's explanation of the photoelectric effect, confirming that light also exhibits particle-like behavior (photons).
  • Bohr Atomic Model (1913): Niels Bohr's introduction of a new model explaining how electrons orbit within an atom using quantum concepts.
  • Modern Quantum Mechanics (1920s): The full establishment of modern quantum mechanics through the development of quantum equations and theories by scientists such as Schrödinger, Heisenberg, Dirac, and Pauli.
